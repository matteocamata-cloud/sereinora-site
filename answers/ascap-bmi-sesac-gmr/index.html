Do I need ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR?

It depends on what you play. ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR are PROs (performing rights organizations). They license songs to be played in public. Each PRO licenses only its own songs, and many songs are split between several of them.

Each PRO licenses only its own songs

All four PROs say the same thing about their licenses:

  • BMI says a license from another PRO “allows you only to perform copyrighted music represented by that organization”. The reason, BMI says, is that “each PRO licenses a unique repertoire of music” (BMI Music Licensing FAQs).
  • SESAC says each organization “licenses only the copyrighted works of its own respective copyright holders” (SESAC FAQ).
  • GMR says licenses with ASCAP, BMI and SESAC “do not grant you authorization” to perform music by GMR’s writers, “and vice versa” (Global Music Rights FAQ).
  • ASCAP says “you need a license with each PRO that represents the creators of the music that you play” (ASCAP Music Licensing FAQs).

So the real question is not “which PRO is the right one”. It is “whose songs are played here, and which licenses cover them”. A curated background music service changes the answer for the music it supplies. See Can I play Spotify in my restaurant or bar?

One song can need more than one license

Songs often have several writers who belong to different PROs. The U.S. Copyright Office notes that the rights to musical works “are often split among multiple songwriters, with differing publishers and PROs” (U.S. Copyright Office, “Copyright and the Music Marketplace” (2015), p. 123). So one song can belong to more than one PRO.

The Department of Justice describes the result. It is called fractional licensing. A PRO licenses only its share of a work, “meaning that a licensee must obtain a license from all of the PROs affiliated with the work (or from the songwriters or publishers directly) in order to legally perform it” (DOJ statement closing its review of the ASCAP and BMI consent decrees (January 15, 2021), p. 2). A licensee is the business that holds the license.

How the four differ

ASCAP and BMI work under long-standing antitrust consent decrees with the Department of Justice. SESAC and GMR do not. Antitrust law is the law on fair competition. A consent decree is an agreement approved by a court. The Copyright Office puts it this way: “neither SESAC nor GMR is subject to such direct antitrust regulation” (Copyright and the Music Marketplace, p. 34). The DOJ notes the decrees “regulate only ASCAP and BMI” (DOJ statement, January 15, 2021, p. 2).

For a venue, the day-to-day point is simpler. Each PRO sets its own fees for bars and restaurants. Each one needs its own license. How much does a music license cost? explains what drives those fees.

Look up which PRO represents a song

Each PRO publishes a free search:

Songview
A shared database. BMI says it covers “the four major PROs, BMI, ASCAP, GMR and SESAC”, with ownership shares per song (Songview at BMI; also reachable from ASCAP’s repertory search).
SESAC
SESAC repertory search: titles, SESAC’s share, and its writers and publishers.
GMR
Global Music Rights search: by title, performer, songwriter or publisher.

A search tells you who represents a song. It does not tell you whether your venue is licensed for it. Searching song by song is also hard to do for a live band or a DJ. They can play hundreds of songs a night.
