Music licensing for hotels: lobby, restaurant, events and rooms

A hotel often has many music uses under one roof. Here is what the PROs’ own documents say about each one, use by use.

On this page

Music in a hotel is a public performance

US copyright law says a work is performed “publicly” when it is performed “at a place open to the public or at any place where a substantial number of persons outside of a normal circle of a family and its social acquaintances is gathered” (17 U.S.C. §101, definitions). Performing rights organizations (PROs) license that right for songwriters and publishers.

SESAC puts it simply for hotels: “If your hotel uses music in guest areas, then you likely need a license” (SESAC, music licensing for hotels). SESAC lists “hotels, motels, resorts, health clubs” among its licensees (SESAC FAQ), and Global Music Rights (GMR) names hotels among the business types it treats differently when setting rates (Global Music Rights FAQ).

Each PRO licenses only its own catalog. ASCAP’s hotel license says its fees “specifically exclude the value” of rights controlled by “Broadcast Music, Inc., SESAC Inc. and Global Music Rights” (ASCAP Musical Works License Agreement for Hotels and Motels, 2022 form, published by AHLA). GMR says licenses with the other three “do not grant you authorization” to perform its writers’ music, “and vice versa” (Global Music Rights FAQ). Do I need ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR? explains how the four fit together.

Hotel music uses, one by one

ASCAP’s hotel license sets out, area by area, how it treats a hotel. It splits music into “Recorded Music Activity” and “Live Entertainment Activity”, and leaves out “Third-Party Licensable Activity”, which it defines in three parts (ASCAP hotel license, 2022 form). The quotes below come from that form unless another source is named. Other PROs’ licenses and later ASCAP versions may be worded differently.

Lobby and background music
Recorded music is any public performance at the hotel “whether in common areas (including gyms and spas) or Lodging Rooms”, by recordings, digital audio, radio or TV. Music from “any jukebox, background music service” is left out of the hotel license where its public performance is “otherwise licensed or licensable” through ASCAP or the Jukebox License Office. SESAC says a background music service’s license “only extends to the music they supply to establishments with no admission, membership or similar charge” (SESAC FAQ).
Restaurant and bar
An outlet the hotel runs itself is part of the premises the hotel license covers. An area “operated by or predominantly under the control of a third party pursuant to a lease, license or other agreement”, where that party runs “a restaurant, bar, retail store, shopping mall, spa or fitness club, or other similar establishment”, is Third-Party Licensable Activity and sits outside the hotel license.
Live music, DJs and karaoke
Live Entertainment Activity is any activity that “features live musicians, singers or other performing artists of music (including, but not limited to, DJs and karaoke hosts)”. GMR says the owner or operator is responsible for the performances on its premises, and paying the performers “will not relieve you of your licensing obligation” (Global Music Rights FAQ). More in Do I need a license for live music, DJs or karaoke?
Ballrooms, meeting rooms and private events
Asked about “meeting/banquet rooms, reception halls, or ballrooms”, SESAC says: “The Copyright Law of the United States and subsequent case law assigns the responsibility for obtaining authorization for copyrighted music played in rented or leased areas to the owner/operator of the establishment” (SESAC FAQ). ASCAP’s hotel form leaves out music “produced, presented, promoted, managed, operated, conducted and/or controlled, in whole or in part, by a third party in connection with any conference, congress, convention, exposition, industrial show, institute, meeting, seminar, teleconference, trade show or other business presentation”, and events where the hotel acts only as agent in a “buy/sell” transaction or mainly provides booking or ticket sales. The two documents describe event space differently, so the license wording and each event contract are where to look.
Gym, spa and fitness
The form names “gyms and spas” among the common areas where recorded music falls under the hotel license, and puts a “spa or fitness club” run by a third party under a lease outside it. Fitness classes taught to music are worth listing as a use of their own.
Music on hold
Recorded music includes “a music-on-hold telephone system operated by Licensee at or for the Premises”. GMR: “Music-on-hold is a type of public performance” (Global Music Rights FAQ).
TVs in rooms and common areas
Recorded music includes “reception and access of radio and/or television (including cable, satellite and any other form of linear or on-demand audiovisual programming)”, in common areas or guest rooms. SESAC says its agreements with cable and satellite providers “do not extend to authorize the public performance of those programs in your business” (SESAC FAQ). The narrow broadcast exemption is explained in Is my venue exempt from music licensing?

How hotel fees are set

In ASCAP’s 2022 hotel form, the recorded music fee for each hotel follows its number of guest rooms (“Lodging Rooms”), and the live entertainment fee follows what the hotels spend on live entertainment over the year. The license covers only the hotels listed in its Schedule A; the licensee’s other hotels “are not covered by or subject to this License Agreement” (ASCAP hotel license, 2022 form). GMR says its rate depends on “your type of business and how the music is presented to the public” (Global Music Rights FAQ), and SESAC says its fees “are determined by relevant industry criteria” (SESAC FAQ).

For a sense of scale, Bloomberg reported this example from the American Hotel & Lodging Association: “A large hotel that hosts occasional live music events could be paying a single PRO $5,000 to $20,000 a year. If it’s paying all of the major PROs, it could be incurring as much as $80,000 in fees, according to the association” (Bloomberg via Indianapolis Business Journal, July 9, 2025). That is the association’s example, not a quote for any hotel. What does a music license cost? covers the fee factors for restaurants and bars.
