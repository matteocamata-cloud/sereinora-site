What happens if a restaurant or bar plays music without a license?

Usually letters and calls come first, and sometimes a federal lawsuit follows. Here is what the PROs and the statute say, step by step.

How it usually unfolds

US copyright law gives the owner of a song the exclusive right “to perform the copyrighted work publicly” (17 U.S.C. §106(4)). Performing rights organizations (PROs) such as ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR license that right to businesses on behalf of songwriters and publishers. The PROs’ own descriptions point to a gradual path:

  1. Letters and calls. ASCAP says that when a bar or club owner refuses a license, it will sue, “but this only happens after many attempts to educate them about their obligations under Federal law” (ASCAP, why ASCAP licenses bars, restaurants and music venues). A BMI spokesperson told Bloomberg that lawsuits are “always a last resort” and that BMI “spends sometimes years on educational outreach” (Bloomberg via Indianapolis Business Journal, July 9, 2025).
  2. A visit. In the same report, BMI said that if those efforts are ignored, “an in-person visit might occur, and BMI may take legal action” (Bloomberg via IBJ).
  3. A lawsuit in federal court. Copyright cases are heard by federal district courts: “No State court shall have jurisdiction” over them (28 U.S.C. §1338(a)). On March 10, 2026, ASCAP announced “11 separate copyright infringement actions against establishments across the country”, and said it had made “numerous attempts” at each one “to offer a license and educate the business owners about their obligations under federal copyright law” (ASCAP press release, March 10, 2026). On October 19, 2026, music publishers and songwriters affiliated with ASCAP filed copyright infringement lawsuits against hospitality and entertainment businesses in several states, and several complaints reportedly allege that PRO representatives contacted the businesses repeatedly before suing (Brownstein client alert, “Music licensing under the microscope” (Nov. 9, 2026)).

A lawsuit concerns specific songs, because statutory damages are set “with respect to any one work” (17 U.S.C. §504(c)(1)). These sources describe the general pattern. They do not say how any single case ends, and neither do we.

What the law allows

Federal copyright law sets out what a court may order when it finds infringement. The quotes below come from the statute itself.

Damages, two ways
An infringer is liable for either the copyright owner’s “actual damages and any additional profits of the infringer”, or statutory damages (17 U.S.C. §504(a)). The copyright owner may choose statutory damages “at any time before final judgment is rendered” (§504(c)(1)).
Statutory damages per work
“Not less than $750 or more than $30,000 as the court considers just”, for each work infringed (17 U.S.C. §504(c)(1)).
If the infringement was willful
Where the copyright owner proves, and the court finds, that the infringement was committed willfully, the court “may increase the award of statutory damages to a sum of not more than $150,000” per work (§504(c)(2)).
If the infringement was innocent
Where the infringer proves, and the court finds, that it “was not aware and had no reason to believe” that its acts were infringing, the court may reduce the award to “not less than $200” per work (§504(c)(2)).
Costs and attorney’s fees
The court “in its discretion may allow the recovery of full costs” and “may also award a reasonable attorney’s fee to the prevailing party as part of the costs” (17 U.S.C. §505).
Injunctions
The court may “grant temporary and final injunctions on such terms as it may deem reasonable to prevent or restrain infringement of a copyright” (17 U.S.C. §502(a)).

These are the ranges the law allows, not a prediction for any venue. Within them, the court decides.

Who may be liable

The PROs say responsibility sits with the business, not only with the DJ or the band. ASCAP: “Anyone involved in enabling an unlicensed public performance may be held liable for copyright infringement, including corporate officers, business owners and managers” (ASCAP Music Licensing FAQs). ASCAP also writes that “all who participate in, or are responsible for, performances of music are legally responsible”, and that “it is the business owner who obtains the license” (ASCAP, why ASCAP licenses bars, restaurants and music venues). SESAC: “a business cannot shift its responsibility to musicians or entertainers” (SESAC, “Who is responsible for paying music licensing fees?”).

These are the PROs’ statements of their position. How liability applies to a particular venue or person is a question for a US attorney. Do I need a license for live music, DJs or karaoke? covers performers in more detail.

What to do

  • If you have been sued, or you have a lawyer’s letter, a demand or a deadline, speak to a US attorney first.
  • Do not ignore letters. A law firm’s advice to hospitality businesses about letters from ASCAP or another rights organization is short: “Do not ignore it” (Brownstein client alert). Got a letter from BMI, ASCAP, SESAC or GMR? sets out what to do this week.
  • Keep a record of what you play. The same alert suggests reviewing music uses across locations and online channels, and confirming that licenses are current and broad enough (Brownstein client alert). List your music service, live nights, DJs, karaoke, TVs and events.
  • Keep the licenses you hold in one place: music service contract, PRO invoices and agreements. Each PRO licenses its own catalog, as Do I need ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR? explains.
