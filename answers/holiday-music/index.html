Do restaurants and bars need a license to play Christmas music?

In most cases, yes. Holiday songs follow the same rules as the rest of your playlist, and even a very old carol is usually heard in a newer version that has its own copyright.

Holiday songs are licensed like any other music

US copyright law gives the owner of a musical work the exclusive right “to perform the copyrighted work publicly” (17 U.S.C. §106(4)). A work is performed “publicly” when it is performed “at a place open to the public or at any place where a substantial number of persons outside of a normal circle of a family and its social acquaintances is gathered” (17 U.S.C. §101, definitions). Neither section treats seasonal music differently from music played the rest of the year.

Many of the best-known holiday songs are recent enough to be under copyright. ASCAP’s 2025 chart of its 25 most-played holiday songs, “all written, co-written or arranged by ASCAP songwriters and composers”, includes “Sleigh Ride” (1948), “Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer” (1949), “White Christmas” (1941) and “All I Want for Christmas Is You” (1994) (ASCAP’s 2025 holiday charts, as reported by BroadwayWorld, December 11, 2025). All of them were written after the public-domain cutoff explained below. Each performing rights organization (PRO) licenses only its own catalog; Do I need ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR? explains how the four fit together.

Old carols: the song, the arrangement and the recording

Copyright in older works does run out. The U.S. Copyright Office states that “all works published in the United States before January 1, 1931, are in the public domain” (U.S. Copyright Office, Circular 15A, revised April 2026). Works published in 1930 entered the US public domain on January 1, 2026 (Duke Center for the Study of the Public Domain, Public Domain Day 2026). The Office also cautions that “there are a number of exceptions” to its general guidance: “In many cases, it is important to consult with a copyright attorney before reaching any conclusions regarding the copyright status of a work” (U.S. Copyright Office, Circular 22, How to Investigate the Copyright Status of a Work, revised April 2026).

What a venue plays is rarely the bare carol. It is a particular arrangement in a particular recording, and each layer is looked at separately:

The song
The words and music as first published. If they were published in the US before 1931, the general rule above applies to them.
The arrangement
The Copyright Act lists a “musical arrangement” as a derivative work (17 U.S.C. §101). Copyright in a new version “extends only to the material contributed by the author of such work” (17 U.S.C. §103(b)). The Copyright Office adds that even when part of a work is in the public domain, “this does not mean that the ‘new’ material added to it can be used without permission from the owner of copyright in the derivative work” (Circular 22). ASCAP’s 2025 chart credits two entries to their arrangers: “Jingle Bells” (Frank Sinatra version, arranged by Gordon Jenkins, 1958) and “Deck the Halls” (Nat King Cole version, arranged by Nat King Cole and Leland Gillette, 1960).
The recording
A recording usually contains two works: “the underlying musical, dramatic, or literary work” and “the fixation of the actual sounds embodying the performance” (Circular 22). Duke’s example: the song “Georgia on My Mind” was published in 1930 and is now free to perform, “But the later recordings by Ray Charles and by Tiny Tim are still copyrighted” (Duke, Public Domain Day 2026). The law frames the performance right in a recording more narrowly than in a song: it covers performing it publicly “by means of a digital audio transmission” (17 U.S.C. §106(6)), and the rights in a sound recording “do not include any right of performance under section 106(4)” (17 U.S.C. §114(a)).

Whether a given track of an old carol is free to play depends on facts that are rarely printed on the playlist: who arranged it, when, and how it reaches your speakers. Searches of Copyright Office records “cannot be regarded as conclusive in all cases” (Circular 22). For a firm answer on a specific track, ask a US copyright attorney.

Holiday playlists, parties and TVs

A personal streaming account
Spotify grants permission for “personal, non-commercial use” (Spotify End User Agreement, section 3). YouTube’s terms say you may listen to content for personal, non-commercial use, and that you may not publicly screen videos or stream music from the service (YouTube Terms of Service). A holiday playlist on a personal plan is under the same terms as any other. More in Can I play Spotify in my restaurant or bar?
Holiday parties, DJs and live bands
SESAC says the owner or operator of an establishment where music is publicly performed needs authorization in advance: “Paying the band does not cover you for that; your business must have authorization.” For rented or leased areas, it says copyright law and case law assign the responsibility “to the owner/operator of the establishment” (SESAC FAQ). GMR says the owner or operator is responsible for the performances on its premises, and paying the performers “will not relieve you of your licensing obligation” (Global Music Rights FAQ). More in Do I need a license for live music, DJs or karaoke?
Holiday radio and TV specials
A narrow exemption covers a transmission “originated by a radio or television broadcast station licensed as such by the Federal Communications Commission” or, for video, by a cable system or satellite carrier, within size and equipment limits (17 U.S.C. §110(5)(B)). Its text names broadcast stations, cable systems and satellite carriers, not streaming services. The limits are explained in Is my venue exempt from music licensing?
