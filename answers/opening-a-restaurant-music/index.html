Opening a restaurant or bar: what music licensing do you need to set up?

Music licensing is one more item on the opening list, and it is easier to set up once than to untangle later. Here is a practical order to work through it, with each step tied to what the PROs and music services say themselves.

On this page

1. List your music uses2. Choose your music source3. Contact each PRO4. Keep recordsNext step

Step 1: List the music uses you plan

Start with a plain list of every way music will be heard in the venue, room by room. The license follows the business: ASCAP says “the business owner is legally responsible to obtain the license” (ASCAP Music Licensing FAQs). Each use below has its own answer page.

Background music
The playlist in the dining room, bar, patio and restrooms. Where it comes from matters; see step 2 and Can I play Spotify in my restaurant or bar?
Live music, DJs and karaoke
ASCAP says live music of any kind, “including DJ nights and karaoke”, needs public performance licenses (ASCAP Music Licensing FAQs). More in Do I need a license for live music, DJs or karaoke?
TVs and radio
A narrow exemption can cover certain radio and TV broadcasts in smaller venues, within equipment limits. It never covers streaming services, live music or music in social videos. See Is my venue exempt from music licensing?
Private events and special nights
A band or DJ booked for a party, a ticketed night or a cover charge is a live use like any other. GMR says paying the performers “will not relieve you of your licensing obligation” (Global Music Rights FAQ).
Social video
Music added to an Instagram, TikTok or YouTube post is a different use from music in the room, with its own platform rules. See Can my venue use popular songs in Instagram or TikTok videos?

Note for each use how often it happens and in which room. That list is what every later step, and every PRO conversation, is built on. How much does a music license cost? explains how these uses feed into fees.

Step 2: Choose your background music source

Consumer streaming plans are written for personal listening. Spotify, for example, grants permission for “personal, non-commercial use” (Spotify End User Agreement, section 3) Playing a personal plan to customers goes beyond those terms.

A business music service is the usual alternative. It generally covers its own recorded catalog played as background music, and not more: Pandora CloudCover says “A background music license also does not cover live performances, cover artists, DJs” (Pandora CloudCover, licensing FAQ). Read your service’s terms for what it includes, and keep a copy. The options are compared in Can I play Spotify in my restaurant or bar?

Step 3: Contact each PRO whose catalog you will play

There are four US performing rights organizations (PROs), and each licenses only its own catalog. ASCAP: “you need a license with each PRO that represents the creators of the music that you play” (ASCAP Music Licensing FAQs). GMR says licenses with the other three “do not grant you authorization” to perform its writers’ music, “and vice versa” (Global Music Rights FAQ). Do I need ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR? explains how the four fit together and how to look up a song.

Each PRO has its own starting point for businesses. Described as the PROs’ own pages showed them on November 13, 2026:

ASCAP
ASCAP’s page for music users lists a “Restaurant, Bar & Nightclub Music License” with a “Purchase License” button. For other businesses it says: “Can’t find your business type? Fill out our licensing contact form and one of our representatives will be in touch with you with the music license that fits your needs” (ASCAP, music licensing for businesses).
BMI
BMI’s FAQ says that whether music is “performed by a live band, DJ or karaoke, the business owner must obtain permission from the copyright owner”, and that a license from another PRO “allows you only to perform copyrighted music represented by that organization” (BMI Music Licensing FAQs).
SESAC
SESAC’s page for bars and restaurants says: “If you use music in your bar or restaurant, then you likely need a license.” It sets out three steps: “Choose your license type”, “Tell us about your business” and “Secure your blanket music license”, adding: “Pay online to secure your music license. For those that cannot, our licensing representatives will follow up to complete the process by email or phone” (SESAC, music licensing for bars and restaurants).
GMR
Global Music Rights says: “A license from Global Music Rights is required to legally play copyrighted musical works (or shares of works) we represent” (Global Music Rights, licensing). Its “Obtain a license” page asks you to select your business type, with “Bars & Restaurants” among them, and to “elaborate on your business type and music performance details” (Global Music Rights, obtain a license).

Have your list from step 1 at hand, with your occupancy and the nights you plan live music, DJs or karaoke. We found no published dollar schedule from BMI, SESAC or GMR for bars and restaurants, so ask each for a written quote based on your occupancy and music use (How much does a music license cost?).

Step 4: Keep records, and recheck when things change

Keep one folder with each license agreement and invoice, your music service contract and terms, and the step 1 list of music uses. It makes renewals simpler and gives you something clear to compare against any letter you receive later. Got a letter from a PRO? covers what to do if one arrives.

Look at the list again whenever the music changes: a first live night, a DJ or karaoke night, a cover charge, private events, more TVs or speakers, or a new music service. A background music service usually stops at background music (step 2), and GMR says its rates “depend upon your type of business and how the music is presented to the public” (Global Music Rights FAQ). A change in how you use music can mean a change in what you need.
