Can my venue use popular songs in Instagram or TikTok videos?

Music in a video you post is a different use from music played in the room. The platforms’ own terms set tighter limits for business and promotional posts than for personal ones.

A different use from the music in your room

The licenses a venue holds for music on its premises are about performing music there. ASCAP’s hotel license, for example, does not grant the right to perform its music “to persons beyond the Premises by any means, methodology or technology”, including “internet-connected websites, software applications, systems, platforms”, and grants no right to “reproduce, copy or distribute” the music or to use the sound recordings (ASCAP Musical Works License Agreement for Hotels and Motels, 2022 form, published by AHLA).

Putting a song into a video involves other rights. SESAC explains that it “does not represent the rights needed to reproduce a musical composition into a video”, that “These rights are generally called sync rights”, and that using an existing recording also means contacting “the record label that owns said recording” (SESAC FAQ). On the performance side, SESAC says “Many major social media platforms are already licensed with SESAC” and that it does not usually license “individual users’ channels or pages” (SESAC FAQ).

The radio and TV exemption for small venues does not help here either: it never covers music added to social media or marketing videos (Is my venue exempt from music licensing?).

What the platforms’ own terms say

Each platform sets rules for the music in its own app. These are the platforms’ words about their own services, not a statement of the law.

Facebook and Instagram (Meta)
Meta’s Music Guidelines apply “if you post, share, or promote any videos or other content containing music on any Meta Products”, which include Instagram (Meta Products). They say: “Use of music for commercial or non-personal purposes in particular is prohibited unless you have obtained appropriate licenses”, and “Nothing in these terms constitutes any authorization by us with respect to any use of music” (Meta Music Guidelines, effective March 26, 2024).
Instagram’s music library
“The music available in our library is intended for personal, non-commercial use”, and “certain business accounts and certain types of posts do not have access to the library” (Instagram Help, access to the licensed music library). A reel you boost as an ad “can’t use licensed music”; Instagram recommends “royalty-free music, like what’s available in our Sound Collection, or original audio” (Instagram Help, how to boost a reel).
TikTok
TikTok’s Commercial Music Library is made available to “brands, businesses, commercial entities and advertisers”. Commercial uses include “Advertising, marketing, endorsing, sponsoring or publicizing” a product or service on TikTok, and “Commercial Sounds are the only sounds made available on TikTok for Commercial Uses.” The separate “General Music Library” is the non-commercial one “made available to personal users of TikTok” (TikTok Commercial Music Library user terms).
YouTube
YouTube’s Audio Library offers “royalty-free production music and sound effects to use in your videos”, and tracks with a Creative Commons license must be credited in the video’s description. YouTube adds: “Only music and sound effects from the Audio Library are known to YouTube to be copyright-safe”, and it “can’t give legal guidance, including guidance on issues with music that can occur off the platform” (YouTube Help, Audio Library).

Options venues commonly use

General information, not a recommendation for any post. Each option comes with its own terms.

  • The platform’s commercial library. Instagram says Meta’s Sound Collection has “over 14,000 songs and sounds” and “can be used for commercial purposes like ads” (Instagram Help). TikTok’s Commercial Music Library stays on TikTok: “Commercial Uses outside of TikTok are not permitted and no rights are granted by TikTok for such uses” (TikTok CML terms). A track picked in one app may not cover the same video posted elsewhere.
  • Music licensed for video. For a specific song, SESAC points to the song’s publishers for sync rights and to the record label for the recording (SESAC FAQ). Music libraries sell licenses too; their terms say which platforms and uses are included.
  • Original audio. Voice-over, your own sound, or no music at all. Instagram lists original audio as an alternative for boosted reels (Instagram Help). A clip filmed during service also records whatever music is playing in the room.

Platform terms change. The versions quoted here were read on November 13, 2026.
