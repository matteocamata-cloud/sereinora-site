Music licensing glossary for venue owners

These are the words you will see in PRO letters, price lists and music service terms, explained in plain English. Each entry names its source and links to the page that explains it.

20 terms, in alphabetical order. A plain-English summary for general information, not legal advice.

A

ASCAP

ASCAP is one of the four groups that give businesses licenses to play songs in public. These groups are called performing rights organizations (PROs). ASCAP says “you need a license with each PRO that represents the creators of the music that you play” (ASCAP Music Licensing FAQs).

It publishes a list of rates for restaurants, bars and nightclubs. Fees are per occupant, which means per person your venue can hold (ASCAP rate schedule, calendar year 2026).

Explained in Do I need ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR?; How much does a music license cost a restaurant or bar?

B

BMI

BMI is one of the four groups that give businesses licenses to play songs in public (PROs). BMI says a license from another PRO “allows you only to perform copyrighted music represented by that organization” (BMI Music Licensing FAQs).

For bars and restaurants, BMI says its license “starts at a little more than a dollar per day”. The price goes up with the type of music, how often it is played, and occupancy (BMI, music licensing for bars and restaurants).

Explained in Do I need ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR?; How much does a music license cost a restaurant or bar?

Business music service

A business music service gives a business recorded music to play in the background, and it licenses that music. Examples are Pandora CloudCover, Mood Media, Rockbot and Soundtrack.

A service usually covers its own recorded music played in public areas. It does not usually cover live performances, cover artists, DJs or karaoke (Pandora CloudCover, licensing FAQ).

Explained in Can I play Spotify in my restaurant or bar?; Do I need a license for live music, DJs or karaoke?

C

Consent decree

A consent decree is a set of rules that ASCAP and BMI have agreed with the Department of Justice to follow. These rules are long-standing, and they come from antitrust law, the law on fair competition. SESAC and GMR are not under them. The DOJ notes the decrees “regulate only ASCAP and BMI” (DOJ statement, January 15, 2021, p. 2). The Copyright Office says “neither SESAC nor GMR is subject to such direct antitrust regulation” (Copyright and the Music Marketplace, p. 34).

Explained in Do I need ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR?

Cover charge (Admission charge)

A cover charge is money a guest pays to get in, such as admission or a cover. ASCAP’s 2026 rate schedule adds a line of $2.72 per occupant for an admission or cover charge (ASCAP rate schedule, calendar year 2026).

It also limits what a music service covers. Soundtrack says that if a business “Charges an admission fee”, the public performance rights “are not licensed by Soundtrack” (Soundtrack General Terms). SESAC says a service’s license “only extends to the music they supply to establishments with no admission, membership or similar charge” (SESAC FAQ).

Explained in How much does a music license cost a restaurant or bar?; Do I need a license for live music, DJs or karaoke?

D

Direct PRO license

A direct PRO license is one a venue gets from a PRO itself, not through a music service. Some venues play music that no licensed service supplies. Others host live music, DJs or karaoke. These venues may need a license from each PRO whose music is played (U.S. Copyright Office, “Copyright and the Music Marketplace” (2015)).

Explained in Can I play Spotify in my restaurant or bar?

E

Enhancements

Enhancements is ASCAP’s word for extras like karaoke, DJs, emcees, dancing, shows, acts or games. It is a line in ASCAP’s rate schedule. For 2026, it adds $2.72 per occupant to the yearly rate (ASCAP rate schedule, calendar year 2026).

Explained in How much does a music license cost a restaurant or bar?

F

Fractional licensing (Split works)

Fractional licensing means each PRO can license only its own share of a song. Songs often have more than one writer, and the writers can belong to different PROs. The rights “are often split among multiple songwriters, with differing publishers and PROs” (U.S. Copyright Office, “Copyright and the Music Marketplace” (2015), p. 123).

So a PRO licenses only its share of a work, “meaning that a licensee must obtain a license from all of the PROs affiliated with the work (or from the songwriters or publishers directly) in order to legally perform it” (DOJ statement, January 15, 2021, p. 2).

Explained in Do I need ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR?

G

GMR (Global Music Rights)

GMR, or Global Music Rights, is one of the four groups that give businesses licenses to play songs in public (PROs). GMR says licenses with ASCAP, BMI and SESAC “do not grant you authorization” to perform music by its writers, “and vice versa”. It also says its rates “depend upon your type of business and how the music is presented to the public” (Global Music Rights FAQ).

Explained in Do I need ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR?; How much does a music license cost a restaurant or bar?

L

Live performance (Live music, DJs and karaoke)

A live performance is music played in your venue by a band, a DJ or karaoke. Each of the four PROs says the license for these shows is the business’s job, not the performer’s (ASCAP, BMI, SESAC, GMR). SESAC puts it this way: “Paying the band does not cover you” (SESAC FAQ).

Business music services usually do not cover live performances (Pandora CloudCover, licensing FAQ).

Explained in Do I need a license for live music, DJs or karaoke?

O

Occupancy

Occupancy is how many people your venue can hold. ASCAP and BMI use this number to set their fees. BMI says the local fire marshal most often sets it (BMI, music licensing for bars and restaurants).

In ASCAP’s 2026 schedule, the yearly fee is a rate per occupant times your venue’s occupancy. If there is no official occupancy, ASCAP uses the square footage divided by 20 (ASCAP rate schedule, calendar year 2026).

Explained in How much does a music license cost a restaurant or bar?

P

Performing rights organization (PRO)

A PRO is a group that gives businesses licenses to play songs in public. It does this on behalf of songwriters and publishers. Bars, restaurants and hotels are among the businesses that get PRO licenses (U.S. Copyright Office, “Copyright and the Music Marketplace” (2015)).

There are four: ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR. Each one represents its own members’ songs. So a venue can need more than one license (SESAC, “What is a performing rights organization?”).

Explained in Do I need ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR?; Got a letter from BMI, ASCAP, SESAC or GMR?

Personal streaming account

A personal streaming account is a music plan for your own listening. Services such as Spotify, Apple Music and YouTube offer them. Their terms limit use to personal listening. Spotify allows “personal, non-commercial use” (Spotify End User Agreement, section 3). Apple allows use “only for personal, noncommercial purposes” (Apple Media Services Terms, section F).

Explained in Can I play Spotify in my restaurant or bar?

PRO letter

A PRO letter is a letter from ASCAP, BMI, SESAC or GMR. A first letter usually offers a license and asks about your venue. Some lead to more contact later.

In October 2026, music publishers and songwriters linked to ASCAP filed lawsuits in several states. They sued hospitality and entertainment businesses for copyright infringement. Several of the complaints reportedly say there was repeated contact about licensing first (Brownstein client alert, “Music licensing under the microscope” (Nov. 9, 2026)).

Explained in Got a letter from BMI, ASCAP, SESAC or GMR?

Public performance

Playing recorded or live music in a business is a public performance. Songwriters are paid for it through performing rights organizations (PROs). These groups give businesses licenses to play songs in public, on behalf of songwriters and publishers (U.S. Copyright Office, “Copyright and the Music Marketplace” (2015)).

Explained in How music licensing works; Got a letter from BMI, ASCAP, SESAC or GMR?

R

Repertory search (Songview)

A repertory search is a free search that shows which PRO represents a song. Each PRO publishes one. Songview is a shared database. BMI says it covers “the four major PROs, BMI, ASCAP, GMR and SESAC”, with ownership shares for each song (Songview at BMI; also reachable from ASCAP’s repertory search). SESAC and GMR publish their own searches (SESAC repertory search, Global Music Rights search).

A search tells you who represents a song, not whether your venue is licensed for it.

Explained in Do I need ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR?

S

SESAC

SESAC is one of the four groups that give businesses licenses to play songs in public (PROs). SESAC says each organization “licenses only the copyrighted works of its own respective copyright holders” (SESAC FAQ). It also says “music licensing fees vary by industry” and by how much music is used (SESAC, how are fees determined?).

Explained in Do I need ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR?; How much does a music license cost a restaurant or bar?

Small-business exemption (17 U.S.C. §110(5)(B))

The small-business exemption is a narrow rule about playing radio or TV in a business. It is found in 17 U.S.C. §110(5)(B), and it covers playing a transmission from an FCC-licensed radio or TV station (or, for video, a cable system or satellite carrier). Under US law, food and drink venues under 3,750 gross square feet, and other businesses under 2,000, have no equipment limits. Larger venues qualify only within limits on loudspeakers, screens and screen size. Other conditions apply in every case, such as no direct charge to see or hear the transmission (17 U.S.C. §110(5), exemption for certain establishments).

It never covers streaming services, downloads, CDs or vinyl, live music, DJs or karaoke, or music added to social media or marketing videos.

Explained in Is my venue exempt from music licensing?

Statutory damages (17 U.S.C. §504(c))

Statutory damages are amounts set by law that a court may award when a copyright is infringed. Under US law (17 U.S.C. §504(c)), a court may award $750 to $30,000 for each work infringed. It may award up to $150,000 if the infringement is willful. It may award as little as $200 if the infringer proves it was innocent (17 U.S.C. §504(c), statutory damages).

That is the range the law allows, not a prediction for any venue.

Explained in Got a letter from BMI, ASCAP, SESAC or GMR?

W

Willful infringement

Willful infringement is infringement that a court finds was willful. When a court finds this, US law allows statutory damages of up to $150,000 per work. That is above the $750 to $30,000 range that applies otherwise (17 U.S.C. §504(c), statutory damages). The range describes the law, not the likely outcome for any venue.
