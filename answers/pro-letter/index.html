Got a letter from BMI, ASCAP, SESAC or GMR?

It is real, it is common, and a first letter does not usually go away on its own. Here is what it means and what to do this week.

On this page

What it isThis weekWhat to avoidHelp

What the letter is

Performing rights organizations (PROs) license the public performance of songs for songwriters and publishers. Bars, restaurants and hotels are among the businesses that take PRO licenses (U.S. Copyright Office, “Copyright and the Music Marketplace” (2015)). ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR each represent their own members’ works, so a venue can need more than one license (SESAC, “What is a performing rights organization?”).

A first letter usually offers a license and asks about your venue. Some go on to follow-up contact. In October 2026, music publishers and songwriters affiliated with ASCAP filed copyright infringement lawsuits against hospitality and entertainment businesses in several states, and several complaints reportedly allege repeated licensing contact before suit (Brownstein client alert, “Music licensing under the microscope” (Nov. 9, 2026)).

For each work infringed, a court may award statutory damages of $750 to $30,000, up to $150,000 if the infringement is willful, and as little as $200 if the infringer proves it was innocent (17 U.S.C. §504(c), statutory damages). That is the range the law allows, not a prediction for your venue.

What to do this week

  1. Note the deadline and who sent it. Check that it is a genuine PRO.
  2. List every music use: background music source, live music, DJs, karaoke, TVs, events, social videos.
  3. Gather what you already pay: music service contract and any PRO invoices.
  4. Check your music service terms. Some services state that their license covers the recorded music they supply, and not live bands, DJs or karaoke (Pandora CloudCover, licensing FAQ).
  5. Check the small-business exemption if you only play radio or TV broadcasts (see the rules).
  6. Reply on time, with facts. If the letter mentions a lawsuit or comes from a lawyer, talk to a US attorney first.

What to avoid

  • Ignoring it. Several lawsuit complaints reportedly describe repeated contact before filing (Brownstein client alert, “Music licensing under the microscope” (Nov. 9, 2026)), so silence tends to prolong the matter rather than end it.
  • Assuming your music service covers live performances. Services generally cover their own recorded catalog, not live bands, DJs or karaoke (Pandora CloudCover, licensing FAQ).
  • Assuming one PRO license covers everything. Each PRO licenses its own members’ works.
  • Switching to a personal streaming account. Spotify’s terms grant “personal, non-commercial use” (Spotify End User Agreement, section 3).
