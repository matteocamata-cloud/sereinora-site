Got a letter from BMI, ASCAP, SESAC or GMR?

The letter is real, and many venues get one. A first letter does not usually go away on its own. Here is what it means and what to do this week.

Rules: United StatesOther countries

On this page

What the letter is

Some groups give businesses licenses to play songs in public. They do this on behalf of songwriters and publishers. These groups are called performing rights organizations, or PROs. Bars, restaurants and hotels are among the businesses that get these licenses (U.S. Copyright Office, “Copyright and the Music Marketplace” (2015)). ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR each represent their own members’ songs. So a venue can need more than one license (SESAC, “What is a performing rights organization?”).

A first letter usually offers a license and asks about your venue. Some lead to more contact later. In October 2026, music publishers and songwriters linked to ASCAP filed lawsuits in several states. They sued hospitality and entertainment businesses for copyright infringement. Several of the complaints reportedly say there was repeated contact about licensing first (Brownstein client alert, “Music licensing under the microscope” (Nov. 9, 2026)).

For each work infringed, a court may award statutory damages of $750 to $30,000. If the infringement is willful, it may award up to $150,000. If the infringer proves it was innocent, the award can be as little as $200 (17 U.S.C. §504(c), statutory damages). That is the range the law allows, not a prediction for your venue.

What to do this week

  1. Note the deadline and who sent it. Make sure it really comes from a PRO.
  2. List every way you use music: where your background music comes from, live music, DJs, karaoke, TVs, events and social videos.
  3. Gather what you already pay: your music service contract and any PRO bills.
  4. Check your music service terms. Some services say their license covers the recorded music they supply. It does not cover live bands, DJs or karaoke (Pandora CloudCover, licensing FAQ).
  5. Check the small-business exemption if you only play radio or TV (see the rules).
  6. Reply on time, with facts. If the letter mentions a lawsuit or comes from a lawyer, talk to a licensed attorney first.

What to avoid

  • Ignoring it. Several lawsuit complaints reportedly describe repeated contact before the lawsuit (Brownstein client alert, “Music licensing under the microscope” (Nov. 9, 2026)). Staying silent tends to make the matter last longer, not end it.
  • Thinking your music service covers live performances. Services usually cover their own recorded music, not live bands, DJs or karaoke (Pandora CloudCover, licensing FAQ).
  • Thinking one PRO license covers everything. Each PRO licenses its own members’ works.
  • Switching to a personal streaming account. Spotify’s terms allow only “personal, non-commercial use” (Spotify End User Agreement, section 3).
