Answers / PRO letters

Got a letter from BMI, ASCAP, SESAC or GMR?

It is real, it is common, and it usually does not go away on its own. Here is what it means and what to do this week.

On this page

What it isThis weekAvoidHelp

01

What the letter is.

Performing-rights organizations (PROs) license the public performance of songs on behalf of songwriters and publishers. ASCAP and BMI represent most US compositions; SESAC and GMR represent smaller catalogues, including well-known writers. Each licenses only its own catalogue, so a venue can need more than one.

A first letter usually offers a license and asks about your venue. Follow-ups can include visits to document what is played. In October 2026, ASCAP-affiliated publishers filed a group of infringement suits against hotels, restaurants and venues in several states after repeated contact.

Statutory damages under US law run from $750 to $30,000 per song, up to $150,000 if infringement is found wilful, plus possible attorneys' fees.

02

What to do this week.

  1. Note the deadline and who sent it. Check it is a genuine PRO.
  2. List every music use: background music source, live music, DJs, karaoke, TVs, events, social videos.
  3. Gather what you already pay: music service contract, any PRO invoices.
  4. Check your music service terms: many include PRO licensing for the recorded background music they supply, but not for live music, DJs or karaoke.
  5. Check the small-business exemption if you only play radio or TV broadcasts (see the rules).
  6. Reply on time, with facts. If the letter mentions a lawsuit or comes from a lawyer, talk to a US attorney first.

03

What to avoid.

  • Ignoring it. Repeated non-response is what precedes many lawsuits.
  • Assuming your music service covers live performances. It usually does not.
  • Assuming one PRO license covers everything.
  • Switching to a personal Spotify account. Personal plans are not business licenses.
