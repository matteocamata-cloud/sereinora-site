1. What plays in your venue

  • Personal Spotify, Apple Music and YouTube accounts are limited by their terms to personal, non-commercial use. Explained in Spotify in a business.

  • ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR each say the license for performances on your premises is the business’s responsibility, not the performer’s. Explained in Live music, DJs and karaoke.

  • Business music services generally do not cover DJs or karaoke, and some exclude nights with an admission charge. Explained in Live music, DJs and karaoke.

  • The exemption for radio and TV broadcasts depends on size and equipment, and never covers streaming, live music, DJs or karaoke. Explained in The small-business exemption.

  • Music in a video you post is a different use from music played in the room, and the broadcast exemption does not cover it. Explained in The small-business exemption.

  • ASCAP and BMI both tie their fees to occupancy. Explained in What a license costs.

2. What you hold

  • A service generally covers its own recorded catalog played in public areas, not live performances, DJs or karaoke. Explained in Spotify in a business.

  • PRO licenses the venue holds directly

    Each PRO licenses only its own members’ works, so one license does not cover another’s songs. Explained in ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR.

  • Some DJ services say their subscription does not cover playing in public. Explained in Live music, DJs and karaoke.

3. Questions to put in writing

Ask each provider in writing, keep the replies with your invoices, and ask before you cancel anything.

To your music service

  • Several services say in writing that live entertainment is outside their license. Explained in Live music, DJs and karaoke.

To each PRO

  • ASCAP and BMI base fees mainly on occupancy and how music is used. Explained in What a license costs.

4. If a letter arrives

  • Several lawsuit complaints reportedly describe repeated licensing contact before filing. Explained in Got a PRO letter?
